Findings

1. LLMs Have Better Priors than Humans

Both humans and LLMs have some priors on grid-like video games before encountering the ARC-AGI-3 tasks. For example, just by looking at the first frame of LS20, one might already infer that it is some kind of maze game in which the player needs to interact with key elements, without taking a single action.

We measure anticipation, where a game mechanism is correctly articulated by the player before it is first triggered in the playthrough. In human trajectories, we found 1,425 mechanism instances with both trigger and recognition annotations, of which only 57 (4.0%) are anticipations. Across the five LLM+harness configurations analyzed, the proportion of anticipations rises to 7.3%–11.3%. This suggests that LLMs anticipate game mechanisms more often than humans do, due to their stronger priors on ARC-AGI-3-like tasks.

2. Humans Use Qualitative Representations

A qualitative representation conveys how a mechanism works, but stops short of the precise conditions and numerical values that would let one implement it as a program. For example, in TU93, a human participant describes the mechanism as: “Now I have to hope that it’s going to follow my path and not go straight into me here.”

On the other hand, LLMs’ descriptions of mechanisms are laden with precise conditions and numerical values. For example, in TU93, OPINE–Claude describes the same enemy’s patrol as: “orange top reversal is ROW 16 (step84), not 22; full bounce rows[16,22,28,34,40] period8 now confirmed at both ends.”

For example, in CN04, a participant attempts to connect shapes while trying to see what happens, gathering information while advancing the goal.

The larger share of “Other / insufficient evidence” for humans (15.3% vs. 0.5%) is mainly due to humans not fully narrating their thoughts while playing, unlike LLMs.

4. Errors & Recovery

Learning an unfamiliar world inevitably involves mistakes, so what matters is whether a learner notices and corrects them. For each type of mistake, we track how many of the total are later successfully recovered from.

Error type (recovered / total)HumansLLM
Incorrect mechanism beliefs11 / 15451 / 593
Failed planning916 / 1,002728 / 822
Failed execution134 / 1366 / 7

These three kinds of error follow the sequence of solving a level. An incorrect mechanism belief occurs when the player infers a wrong mechanism and commits to this belief. Failed planning occurs when an attempt does not achieve its intended goal or subgoal. Failed execution occurs when the player states exactly what they intend to do but their actual input differs, such as clicking just outside a control.

The gap in incorrect mechanism beliefs (15 for humans vs. 593 for LLMs) suggests a difference in how the two handle uncertainty. Humans explicitly say they don’t understand a rule 79 times, compared with 29 times for LLMs. When unsure, humans tend to hold off rather than commit, whereas LLMs commit to a belief anyway and later revise it (451 of 593 recovered). Part of the gap also reflects that LLMs write out every conclusion, while humans do not narrate every change in understanding.

Humans are also prone to execution lapses such as misclicks: we record 136 failed executions for humans, compared with only 7 for LLMs. These lapses invariably bloat the number of actions in a human trajectory. This matters for scoring, since LLM actions are measured against human trajectory length.

5. LLMs Verbalize Far More than Humans

Over a successful trajectory, a human’s think-aloud averages about 1,200 words (≈1.4k tokens), while an LLM’s public reasoning averages about 18,000 words (≈29k tokens), roughly 15–20× more. Per action, the gap is even larger: 1.2 words for humans versus about 50 for LLMs. While this is unsurprising, it does reveal how differently humans and LLMs reason.

Implications for Benchmark Design

Our findings point to three directions for future interactive benchmarks. First, because LLMs have strong priors on grid-like games (e.g., they can easily write code for them), tasks could feature mechanisms that emerge from interactions during the execution of the game, rather than being encoded explicitly in the code itself (e.g., Conway’s Game of Life). Second, humans tend to form abstract, qualitative world models, which offer little advantage in ARC-AGI-3, where precise, mechanistic models of the rules are simply too effective; more stochastic environments could better reveal the value of qualitative abstraction. Finally, humans make far more execution errors than LLMs, which inflates their action counts; scoring could be adjusted so that such lapses are not counted as harshly.
