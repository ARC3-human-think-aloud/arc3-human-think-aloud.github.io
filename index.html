Abstract

Learning through exploration and using newly acquired knowledge to achieve goals are central capabilities of general intelligence. Understanding these capabilities requires tracing how learners build, use, and revise world models during interaction. We construct the first dataset that records how humans discover and use the mechanisms of novel environments, using ARC-AGI-3 as a testbed: 144 trajectories from 64 participants across 25 public games, each pairing time-synchronized actions with think-aloud audio, plus 250 trajectories from large language model (LLM) agents with and without harnesses on the same games. We annotate mechanism triggers and recognition, action purposes, and errors and recovery, linking changes in understanding to the observations and actions that support them. We analyze characteristics of human cognition and compare them with those of LLM agents. Both groups anticipate mechanisms and use familiar analogies. Qualitative representations are more common in successful human trajectories than in successful LLM trajectories (50.0% versus 14.3%). Humans more often explore unfamiliar situations without a concrete hypothesis, while hypothesis testing is common in both groups. We also trace how learners recover through belief revision, replanning, and execution correction. As LLMs with well-designed harnesses reach saturated performance on public ARC-AGI-3 games, our cognitive analyses motivate next-generation benchmarks that challenge familiar priors, require accumulated evidence and abstraction, and test planning through subgoals, experimentation, and recovery.

Findings

1. Humans Draw on Prior Knowledge

Of 1,425 human mechanism instances with both trigger and recognition annotations, 57 (4.0%) show evidence of correct understanding before the first trigger. Across the five LLM+harness configurations analyzed, this proportion is 7.3%–11.3%. Prior knowledge helps both groups anticipate the rules of unfamiliar environments.

For example, a participant in TU93 identifies the green endpoint as the goal and moves toward it before reaching it.

Analogies occur in 37/144 (25.7%) of human trajectories and 26/119 (21.8%) of successful LLM trajectories. For example, a human interprets an object in BP35 as a Mario-style pipe, then rejects that interpretation after feedback; LLMs draw on peg solitaire or Lights Out to interpret games.

2. Humans Use Qualitative Representations

Qualitative representations describe how a mechanism works without fully specifying its conditions, timing, or numerical details. For example, a participant in TU93 plans a route using the idea that the enemy follows their path, without specifying the movement delay.

Implications for Benchmark Design

Counterfactual and Emergent Mechanisms

Introduce rules that violate familiar expectations, or define only local dynamics so that higher-level mechanisms emerge from interactions among components. Learners must discover the actual rules through experimentation and construct and test combinations that produce useful outcomes.

Induction through Accumulated Evidence and Abstraction

For instances with both trigger and recognition annotations, the mean action gap between the first trigger and first recognition is 90.8 for humans and 24.4 for LLM+harness agents. Future tasks could introduce hidden states, stochastic outcomes, and delayed effects to require evidence accumulated across interventions. More complex environments could make complete, precise modeling impractical, encouraging qualitative abstractions suited to the current goal.

Useful Subgoals and Testing Combined Outcomes

In levels meeting the analysis criteria, humans still require an average of 95.1 actions to finish after recognizing all relevant main mechanisms, compared with 49.2 for LLM+harness agents. Future tasks could require longer plans and deeper subgoal dependencies, such as building an automated sorting and transport system: decomposing functions, constructing components, testing how they work together, and revising plans after failures.
